Overview

Serve a super hungry cat by fulfilling its bizarre burger orders while surviving a stall where shutters close unexpectedly, ingredient colors get distorted, and buttons swap places in chaotic fast-paced gameplay.

What I Built

⚙️

Core Gameplay Systems

Designed and coded the full order-generation loop in C# — randomised ingredient combinations, a timed serving window, and a score multiplier that rewards consecutive correct orders.

🐛

The Malfunction Mechanic

Designed a "broken stall" system that randomly swaps ingredients mid-order, forcing players to stay alert on every serve. What started as a playtesting observation became the game's signature twist.

🎨

All Pixel Art Assets

Drew every sprite from scratch in Aseprite — the cat character, all burger ingredients, UI frames, and animation frames — while iterating art and code in parallel so readability issues surfaced immediately.

📱

Mobile Build & Publishing

Configured the Android build pipeline end-to-end in Unity, handled signing and APK packaging, and navigated the Google Play Console publishing flow for the first time.

Thought Process

Start with one mechanic, not five

With a month on the clock my first instinct was to cut. I landed on a single verb — serve — because one mechanic made deep through randomisation would be more satisfying than several shallow ones. The cat customer gave the stakes a personality without needing any extra systems.

Turn a playtest problem into a feature

Early playtesting showed the order loop felt predictable after a few rounds. Instead of adding new order types, I introduced the malfunction mechanic — random ingredient mix-ups the player must catch and correct. It reuses the existing system entirely while adding a new layer of tension. The "bug" became the hook.

Art and code in parallel, not sequence

I drew assets alongside coding rather than after — if a sprite wasn't readable at a glance it got redrawn immediately. Working both roles at once meant visual and interaction feedback loops stayed tight, and I never hit the end of development with a pile of unreadable art to redo.

Scope is the real design skill

The hardest calls were what to cut. Trello kept remaining tasks visible against the deadline so I could make those calls deliberately rather than by running out of time. I shipped a complete, polished experience over a technically impressive but unfinished one — that priority is something I carry into every project now.

Reflection

Owning every role taught me where the real bottlenecks are. For me it wasn't code or art — it was decision fatigue from constant context-switching. Trello helped, but the bigger lesson was that ruthless scoping is the most critical skill in solo development.

This project gave me an end-to-end map of what shipping actually requires. Every team project since, I've contributed more deliberately because I understand what each role genuinely costs.
